01

The Problem

Renting and property management workflows are often messy and inefficient.

Tenants struggle to find verified listings, track payments, or get updates on maintenance issues. Landlords manage listings and tenant communication with little visibility or structure. Property managers deal with manual records, poor communication, and limited insight into their portfolios.

These disconnected experiences lead to delays, misunderstandings, and unnecessary stress for everyone involved.

  • Centralizes property and tenant information
  • Improves communication across all stakeholders
  • Simplifies payments, leases, and maintenance tracking
  • Provides real-time visibility and insights
  • Remains intuitive and easy to use, regardless of role
02

Research

To get past assumptions about what tenants, landlords, and property managers actually needed, I started with direct conversations rather than a single stakeholder's view of the problem.

I ran in-person interviews across all three user types: 2 tenants, 2 property managers, and 1 landlord, each walking through how they currently handle rent, leases, maintenance, and communication. That was paired with a review of comparable rental and property management platforms already on the market, and with domain knowledge from working inside the property management space day to day.

  • In-person interviews: 2 tenants, 2 property managers, 1 landlord
  • Competitive review of existing rental & property management platforms
  • Domain knowledge from working directly in the property management space
03

Synthesis

The interviews split cleanly into two sets of friction, one for landlords and property managers, one for tenants. Mapping out each group's current-state journey made the priority order clear.

For landlords and property managers, the biggest friction points were collecting rent payments, tracking tenancy agreements, and screening tenants, all handled manually with little structure. For tenants, the friction was upfront rent payments, and having no clear channel to raise maintenance requests or communicate with landlords and property managers.

  • Rent collection & tracking (landlords, property managers)
  • Tenancy agreement management (landlords, property managers)
  • Tenant screening (landlords, property managers)
  • Upfront rent payments (tenants)
  • Maintenance requests & communication (tenants)

My Role

As the lead product designer on a two-person design team, I set the overall design direction and did the bulk of the hands-on design work — research, information architecture, and interface — working directly with the founding team and my co-designer to take the platform from concept to a live product now used by tenants, landlords, and property managers.

One of the harder calls was designing three genuinely different experiences (tenant, landlord, property manager) without fragmenting the product into three separate apps. That meant building a shared design system flexible enough to hold role-specific complexity without losing consistency — and clear enough that both of us on the design side could build within it consistently.

Collaboration & Handoff

A two-person design team (myself and another product designer) working closely with engineers throughout, not just at handoff. Design QA after implementation, reviewing the build against Figma and resolving inconsistencies directly with engineers, was a core part of getting each release into production.

06

Business Impact

  • Unified three distinct user roles, tenant, landlord, and property manager, into a single coherent product instead of three disconnected tools
  • Delivered as a live product now used by tenants, landlords, and property managers
  • Built and led by a two-person design team, with a shared design system enabling consistent scale across roles

Reflection

Rezidant sharpened how I think about designing for multiple user roles inside one product without fragmenting the experience — the easy answer is three separate tools; the harder, better one is a single flexible system.

If I were starting over, I'd push for role-based user testing earlier — some of the sharpest usability issues only surfaced once real property managers were in the flow. That's shaped how I sequence design work now: validate with the most demanding user role first, then simplify outward.
